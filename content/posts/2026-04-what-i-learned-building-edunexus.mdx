When I started EduNexus, I had a diagram. Teachers on one side, students on the other, my software in the middle like a helpful bridge. Twelve months later I had learned the first real lesson of building for education: nobody wants to live on a bridge.<Footnote n={1}>The diagram survives in my notebook as a warning. It has arrows. So many arrows.</Footnote>

The platform did the expected things — classrooms, lessons, quizzes, the machinery of a teaching term. The interesting problems were never the features. They were the moments where I had to decide what the software *believed*: does a quiz exist to rank students or to tell the teacher what to reteach? The database schema is the same either way. Everything else about the product is not.

> The schema is the same either way. The product is not.

## The teach-back moment

The feature I'm proudest of wasn't in the original plan. Watching classmates test the app, I noticed students understood material best when they had to explain it — so we built a mode where the student teaches the concept back, and the system listens rather than lectures.<Footnote n={2}>The pedagogy people call this the protégé effect. I called it "the thing that finally made the demo interesting."</Footnote> It reframed the whole project for me. The best educational software isn't a better teacher. It's a more patient audience.

## What I'd do differently

Less time on the dashboard, more time in classrooms. I built charts no teacher asked for because charts photograph well in a dissertation.<Footnote n={3}>They did photograph well. My examiners asked about the teach-back mode.</Footnote> The honest metric for education software isn't engagement — it's whether the room needs you less next week than it did last week.

I'm still not sure software can teach. I'm fairly sure it can get out of the way better than it currently does. That feels like a career's worth of problem, which is convenient, because I'm looking for exactly that.
